---
title: Multidimensional scaling
description: Place cases on a map so that distances reflect how different they are.

## When to use it

Use multidimensional scaling when you want a picture of cases, not a picture of variables. People who gave similar answers land near each other. Two dimensions is the usual map. This is not a factor analysis of the items.

## Assumptions

Columns are numeric. Distance is Euclidean after listwise deletion. The request does not standardize the columns, so a column with a much larger scale dominates the distances. You need at least two more complete rows than the number of dimensions.

## Running it in Tensr

<Steps>
<Step>Analyze → Scale → Multidimensional Scaling. In chat: “MDS of these four items in two dimensions.”</Step>
<Step>Select at least two columns. Dimensions default to 2, from 1 to 10.</Step>
</Steps>

## Reading the output

Eighty people and the same four correlated items used for Cronbach’s alpha. Two dimensions.

| N | Dimensions | Stress |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 80 | 2 | 475.272 |

The first case is placed at (−4.706, −1.403). Each later row of `coordinates` is another person, in the same order as the complete cases.

Stress here is the raw sum of squared gaps between the map distances and the original distances. It is not a 0-to-1 index. A larger raw stress means a worse match, but the number grows with the number of people and with the scale of the variables, so 475 is not “475 times too big.” Compare stress only across maps of the same data.

## Reporting (APA 7)

Metric MDS placed 80 cases in two dimensions from four items (raw stress = 475.27).

## Coming from SPSS

Analyze → Scale → Multidimensional Scaling (PROXSCAL / ALSCAL). SPSS stress is usually scaled to a 0-to-1 range. Tensr reports the unscaled stress from the fit.
